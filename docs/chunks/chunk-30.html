Theme: 13. Selective Populism

"Left to our own devices, the American people rejected European monarchy and colonialism just as we rejected slavery, second-class citizenship for women, mercantilism, socialism, Wilsonian globalism, Fascism, Communism, and (today) wokeism."

The text defines 'the American people' as a monolithic entity characterized by a specific set of ideological rejections. By grouping 'wokeism' and 'globalism' with 'Fascism' and 'Communism' as things 'the people' inherently reject, it implies that citizens who hold those views are not part of the legitimate 'People,' but are instead part of the 'ruling elite' or 'the Left' who disdain the nation.

Theme: 14. Ur-Fascism Speaks Newspeak

"The promise of socialism—Communism, Marxism, progressivism, Fascism, whatever name it chooses—is simple: Government control of the economy can ensure equal outcomes for all people."

This passage collapses distinct and often diametrically opposed political ideologies (such as Fascism and Marxism) into a single, oversimplified category ('socialism'). This rhetorical move limits the capacity for complex or critical reasoning by stripping these terms of their specific historical and theoretical meanings, creating a simplified 'elementary syntax' for political discourse.

Theme: 6. Appeal to Social Frustration

"It’s this radical equality—liberty for all—not just of rights but of authority—that the rich and powerful have hated about democracy in America since 1776. They resent Americans’ audacity in insisting that we don’t need them to tell us how to live."

The text weaponizes status anxiety by framing political and institutional friction as a matter of personal 'resentment' and 'hatred' from a 'ruling class.' It tells the reader they are being looked down upon and disdained for their 'audacity,' mobilizing the follower through a narrative of class-based humiliation rather than specific policy disagreement.
